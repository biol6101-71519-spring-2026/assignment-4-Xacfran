# De Novo Hybrid Genome Assembly

## Introduction

I performed a *de novo* hybrid genome assembly of *Helicobacter pylori*, which is a Gram-negative bacterium that inhabits the gastric mucosa and is highly relevant to public health because of its association with chronic gastritis, peptic ulcers, and gastric cancer. For this purpose, I downloaded Illumina HiSeq paired-end short reads and PacBio RS II long reads, obtained from the NCBI SRA (BioProject PRJNA349024 and SRX2250912, respectively). I chose a hybrid assembly strategy due to the high accuracy of Illumina reads to a base level, and together with the contiguity provided by PacBio reads, this approach is known to resolve highly repetitive regions. I chose SPAdes as the assembler because it supports hybrid mode with PacBio reads and is known to perform well on small bacterial genomes.

## Methods

### Data and Quality Control (QC)

The quality of the raw Illumina reads were assessed with FastQC v0.12.1, and PacBio reads with NanoPlot v1.41.0 (De Coster et al. 2018). Aggregate QC reports were generated with MultiQC v1.14. The raw Illumina data showed moderate quality, and adapter contamination, with GC content ~39%. PacBio reads had a median length of 999 bp and mot per sequence quality scores were around Q1, and only ~10,000 around Q9.

### Preprocessing

Illumina reads were trimmed with fastp v0.23.4 (Chen et al. 2018) using a minimum quality threshold of Q20, a minimum length of 50 bp, and sliding-window trimming from both ends. Moreover, the adapters were set to be auto-detected and removed. PacBio reads were filtered with Filtlong v0.2.1 (Wick 2021), and only reads greater or equal to 1,000 bp were retained, keeping the top 90% by quality. This strategy was preferred to remove very short fragments that add noise while ensuring enough data for good coverage.

### Assembly

The hybrid assembly was performed with SPAdes v3.15.5 (Bankevich et al. 2012) in careful mode, providing the trimmed Illumina reads as paired-end input and the filtered PacBio reads. SPAdes automatically selects k-mer sizes and uses the long reads to scaffold the short-read *de Bruijn* graph. The assembly was run with default parameters, except for the --careful flag to reduce mismatches and indels.

### Quality Assessment

QUAST v5.2.0 (Gurevich et al. 2013) was used to obtain assembly statistics like: N50, total length, contig count, and GC content.  BUSCO v5.5.0 (Simao et al. 2015) was then implemented to assess completeness, utilizing the campylobacterales_odb10 lineage. As an additional check of assembly quality, the trimmed Illumina reads were mapped back to the assembly with minimap2 v2.26 (Li 2018), and coverage per contig was calculated with samtools v1.17.

### Read Mapping Coverage

Mean coverage across the assembly was highly variable, ranging from 0X to over 500,000X depending on contig size. The larger contigs had average depth between 50 and 600X. However, the vast majority of the 4,412 contigs were short fragments (250-280 bp) with coverage of only 1-3X.


## Discussion

The total assembly length achieved here is close to the expected genome size of *H. pylori* (~1.67 Mb). However, all assembly statistics are quite concerning regarding the quality of it. The N50 of ~2 kbp is very low for a bacterial genome. It consists of many small contigs rather than a few large ones, and the coverage of each one of this is of a very low quality. This indicates that the assembly is highly fragmented, more than would be expected for a complete genome and barely publishable.

The BUSCO scores indicate that most single-copy orthologs are present (94.1% complete), and there are a few missing and fragmented genes, which could be due to the fragmentation of the assembly or gaps in coverage. A typical *H. pylori* genome contains around 1,500-1,700 genes, so the presence of 591 complete BUSCOs is a good sign of gene content completeness, even if the assembly is fragmented.

This work should be improved by firstly ensuring that the long and short reads are generated by the same lab and cone from the same project. Although both were sequenced from the same bacterial strain, genetic variations are still expected and could result in differences in the assembly. This might be the reason why I obtain a highly fragmented asembly, yet with a high BUSCO score. There is a very low probability that this is improved by polishing with Pilon, but the most optimal solution would be to use the same data for both long and short reads.

In this work, most challenges were encountered while setting up the pipeline's environments and troubleshooting errors, more than understanding the tools and interpreting the results. This dependencies problems were resolved by a mixture of consulting with peers, professor, and stackoverflow.
